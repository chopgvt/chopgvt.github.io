Why Calculators Matter in School

Calculators take care of the slow, repetitive part of arithmetic so students can focus on understanding the problem in front of them. When a long multiplication or a messy decimal no longer eats up the lesson, more time goes to the real learning: deciding which operation to use, setting up an equation, and checking whether an answer makes sense. This is especially useful in subjects like algebra, geometry, physics and chemistry, where the calculation is only one small step in a bigger idea.

Research supports using them sensibly. A widely cited 2003 meta-analysis of calculator studies in school mathematics found that students who used calculators as part of their lessons generally kept their basic skills and tended to do better at problem solving than students who did not. The National Council of Teachers of Mathematics also supports calculator use alongside, not instead of, learning to calculate by hand. Calculators are also allowed or even required in many school and college exams, so learning to use one properly is a practical skill in its own right.

Calculators also help students build confidence and accuracy. Getting a quick, correct result lets learners test their own estimates, spot mistakes, and try out several “what if” ideas in a few seconds, which makes exploring numbers more fun and less stressful. For students who find arithmetic frustrating, a calculator can keep them engaged with the subject instead of giving up on it. The best approach is a balance: practise mental maths and written methods to understand how numbers work, and use a calculator to handle larger or more complex problems quickly and correctly.

Different kinds of calculators suit different stages of school. Basic calculators cover everyday arithmetic, scientific calculators add functions such as powers, roots, logarithms and trigonometry for older students, and graphing calculators can plot equations so learners can see how changing a number changes a curve. Seeing a graph appear instantly often makes abstract ideas in algebra and calculus much easier to picture.

Calculators can also make lessons fairer and more flexible. Students who find arithmetic especially difficult are sometimes given calculators as a support, so they can show what they understand about the bigger concept without being held back by the sums. Teachers can also use them to set more realistic, real-world problems, such as working with measurements, money or data, where the numbers are rarely small and tidy.

A Short History of Calculators

People have been using tools to help with arithmetic for thousands of years. The abacus, a frame of beads used to count and add, appeared in several ancient cultures and is still used in some parts of the world today. In the early 1600s, the Scottish mathematician John Napier published his work on logarithms, which turned hard multiplication into simpler addition, and soon afterwards the slide rule was developed. Slide rules were carried by engineers and students for around 350 years, right up until electronic calculators replaced them in the 1970s.

The first mechanical calculating machines came in the 1600s. In 1642, a teenage Blaise Pascal built an adding machine with geared wheels to help his father, who worked as a tax collector. A few decades later, Gottfried Leibniz designed a machine that could also multiply and divide. In the 1800s, machines such as the arithmometer became the first calculators sold in large numbers to businesses, and Charles Babbage drew up plans for even more ambitious engines that are often seen as early ancestors of the modern computer.

Electronic calculators arrived in the 1960s and were first large, expensive desk machines. Over the early 1970s, advances in microchips shrank them to pocket size, and prices fell quickly. In 1972, Hewlett-Packard launched the HP-35, widely regarded as the first handheld scientific calculator, and the cheap scientific models that followed became common in classrooms. In 1985, Casio released the fx-7000G, the first graphing calculator, and today the same basic function is built into almost every phone and computer.

How Calculators Are Made

A modern calculator is built around a small integrated circuit, a tiny chip that does all the thinking. Inside, numbers are stored and processed in binary, the ones and zeros that electronic circuits understand, and many calculators use a special decimal-friendly form of binary so that results such as 0.1 + 0.2 come out the way people expect. The chip is usually mounted directly onto a flat green circuit board and covered with a small drop of protective black resin.

The keys sit on a rubber or plastic mat, and under each key is a small conductive pad. Pressing a key connects two traces on the circuit board, and the chip scans the keypad many times a second to work out which button was pressed. The results appear on a liquid crystal display (LCD), where each digit is made from seven small segments that switch on and off to form the numbers 0 to 9. Early calculators used glowing LED displays, which used a lot of power, so the switch to low-power LCDs was a big step forward.

Because LCDs and modern chips use so little energy, many calculators can run on a tiny solar cell, sometimes with a small backup battery. The case is typically made from plastic shaped in a mould, and the whole thing is assembled in factories, often on automated lines where the circuit board, keypad, display and case are put together and tested. The result is a device that is light, cheap and rugged, but still able to do millions of operations faster than any person could.
